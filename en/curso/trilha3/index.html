🥊 Red team and robustness

The 10 commands in family 04 — adversarial challenge, failure simulation, and strengthening under uncertainty.

What it is:

The protocol requires you to refute or challenge the premise before agreeing—disagreement comes first, support comes later (if it holds up).

Why learn:

Models are complacent by default; /pushback turns agreement into something that must be earned.

Key concepts:

refutation first · agreement earned · antidote to flattery.

What it is:

Attacks the central thesis with the strongest possible objections and asks for an explicit defense of each.

Why learn:

A thesis that has never been challenged is a hypothesis with borrowed confidence.

Key concepts:

strong objection · explicit defense · tested thesis.

What it is:

Simulates a capable adversary trying to bring down a plan, system, or argument by exploiting real vulnerabilities.

Why learn:

It’s cheaper to find the failure in the simulator than in the market.

Key concepts:

attack vector · vulnerability · mitigation.

What it is:

Put the plan through extreme conditions (/stress) and map failure modes with detection and mitigation (/failure).

Why learn:

Every plan has a load limit; it’s better to learn it in a test than on a bad Tuesday.

Key concepts:

extreme condition · failure mode · detection trigger.

What it is:

Assumes the project has ALREADY failed and works backward: what were the likely causes?

Why learn:

Thinking of the future as the past frees the team to say the unthinkable — risks become legitimate.

Key concepts:

prior retrospective · plausible cause · early mitigation.

What it is:

Actively look for the case that overturns the general rule — the exception that refutes or limits it.

Why learn:

One well-documented exception is worth more than a hundred confirming examples.

Key concepts:

negative case · rule delimitation · honest scope.

What it is:

Identify cognitive biases in the reasoning (/bias) and areas the analysis hasn’t even considered (/blindspots).

Why learn:

Confirmation bias and survivorship bias silently distort even technically impeccable analyses.

Key concepts:

confirmation bias · survivorship bias · unexamined area.

What it is:

Redesigns the solution to work reasonably well across several scenarios—not perfectly in just one.

Why learn:

Robustness trades a fragile optimum for something good that can withstand the real world.

Key concepts:

multi-scenario performance · hedge · margin of safety.

3.2~75 min

🎯 Comparison and decision

The 10 commands in family 05 — criteria, weights, rankings, matrices, and bets with a stopping point.

What it is:

Set measurable criteria and their weights BEFORE evaluating the options.

Why learn:

The criterion chosen after the favorite option is selected magically adjusts so that option wins.

Key concepts:

a priori criterion · relative weight · fixed yardstick.

What it is:

Sorts options by criteria and explains each ranking — a ranking without justification is just numbered preference.

Why learn:

It’s the most commonly used decision command in day-to-day work; with parameters, it becomes a team standard.

Key concepts:

ordering · positional rationale · criteria=impact,cost,risk.

What it is:

Assigns scores by criterion on a defined scale, showing how the total score is composed.

Why learn:

A composite score reveals whether the win came from one dominant criterion or broad consistency.

Key concepts:

anchor scale · score per criterion · transparent weighting.

What it is:

Produces a side-by-side comparison by dimension — without forcing a premature verdict.

Why learn:

Separating comparison from decision keeps a first impression from closing the window for analysis.

Key concepts:

comparable dimension · suspension of judgment.

What it is:

Close the process: one recommendation with evidence, degree of reversibility, and cost of changing your mind.

Why learn:

Reversible decisions call for speed; irreversible ones call for depth. Knowing which is which changes everything.

Key concepts:

two-way door · cost of reversal · clear commitment.

What it is:

Build the full matrix: evaluate each alternative against every criterion, applying the weights.

Why learn:

The matrix exposes trade-offs hidden behind polished narratives.

Key concepts:

weighted matrix · justified cell · sensitivity.

What it is:

Eliminates dominated options and presents the frontier where no criterion can improve without worsening another.

Why learn:

Half the "options" on any list shouldn't be there—ruling them out saves entire meetings.

Key concepts:

dominated option · Pareto frontier · efficiency.

What it is:

/bet structures the bet: upside, downside, size, and triggers; /stop defines the objective condition for ending it without drama.

Why learn:

A bet without a stop-loss becomes a zombie project — kept alive by pride and costing a lot every month.

Key concepts:

return asymmetry · kill criteria · graceful exit.
