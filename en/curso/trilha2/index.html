🛰️ Research and discovery

The 10 commands in family 02 — structured investigation, scanning, discovery, and trend analysis.

What it is:

Research with defined questions, evidence gathering, synthesis of findings, and a conclusion that identifies gaps and next steps.

Why learn:

It’s the default command for any new topic — it replaces ten scattered searches with a process.

Key concepts:

research question · evidence · stated gap.

What it is:

Expands sources, triangulates evidence, compares interpretations, and documents relevant conflicts between them.

Why learn:

High-stakes decisions call for paid depth: triangulation is what separates research from informed opinion.

Key concepts:

triangulation · documented conflict · depth cost.

What it is:

Searches broadly for emerging signals, players, solutions, and developments without diving too deep too soon.

Why learn:

Avoids the classic mistake of digging deep in the wrong place because the first hypothesis seemed promising.

Key concepts:

breadth before depth · signal · candidate for a deep dive.

What it is:

Look for patterns, adjacencies, combinations, and underexplored areas that conventional search doesn't reveal.

Why learn:

Competitive advantage lies in what’s obvious to few—discovering combinations is a skill you can train.

Key concepts:

adjacency · unusual combination · whitespace.

What it is:

Identifies missing questions, necessary data, risks, exceptions, and overlooked opportunities.

Why learn:

The question nobody asked is often worth more than everyone else’s answers combined.

Key concepts:

missing question · exception · overlooked opportunity.

What it is:

/trends distinguishes fads, sustained growth, emerging signals, and structural change; /signals looks for early clues that anticipate change.

Why learn:

Betting on a fad as if it were a structural shift is the costliest strategy mistake.

Key concepts:

trend taxonomy · weak signal · time horizon.

What it is:

Projects alternative future scenarios to test the robustness of plans and bets.

Why learn:

A plan that only works in the optimistic scenario isn't a plan — it's wishful thinking.

Key concepts:

scenario · key uncertainty · robustness test.

What it is:

/benchmark compares performance against market benchmarks; /landscape maps the full ecosystem of players, forces, and moves.

Why learn:

Without a map of the landscape, you're competing against your own assumptions about the competition.

Key concepts:

reference · ecosystem · relative position.

🧪 Analysis and synthesis

The 10 commands in family 03 — breaking things down, modeling, separating cause from correlation, and simplifying without losing the essence.

What it is:

Breaks the problem into analyzable parts using an explicit method.

Why learn:

A poorly decomposed problem produces a well-written answer to the wrong question.

Key concepts:

decomposition · explicit method · subproblem.

What it is:

Integrates multiple findings into a coherent view, keeping conflicts visible instead of dissolving them into an average.

Why learn:

Poor synthesis erases exactly the information that mattered: the differences between sources.

Key concepts:

integration · tension preserved · single view.

What it is:

Builds an explanatory model of how the system being studied works—variables, relationships, and dynamics.

Why learn:

A good model predicts what happens when you change a variable; a list of facts predicts nothing.

Key concepts:

variable · proposed causal relationship · predictive power.

What it is:

Creates a reusable structure for analyzing cases in the same family (dimensions, axes, criteria).

Why learn:

Framework turns one-off analysis into a permanent team capability.

Key concepts:

dimension · axis · analytical reuse.

What it is:

Separates correlation from causation, proposes causal mechanisms, and tests alternative explanations.

Why learn:

Acting on correlation without a causal mechanism is a gamble—with a company’s budget.

Key concepts:

mechanism · confounding factor · rival explanation.

What it is:

Tracks consequences of consequences—the second-order effects that arrive late and hit harder.

Why learn:

Every incentive creates a counterincentive; anyone who ignores second-order effects is surprised by them.

Key concepts:

indirect effect · delay · feedback.

What it is:

/tradeoffs spells out what you gain and give up with each option; /constraints maps the real constraints (time, money, regulation, people).

Why learn:

A proposal without a stated tradeoff hides the cost somewhere—usually in the future.

Key concepts:

concession · hard constraint · possible boundary.

What it is:

/leverage finds the points where little effort produces disproportionate change; /simplify reduces complexity while preserving the essential function.

Why learn:

Simplification is the most elegant way to improve performance—and the most overlooked.

Key concepts:

leverage point · essential function · deliberate cut.
